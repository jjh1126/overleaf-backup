\begin{abstract}
	
	In online virtual network embedding (VNE), an embedding must accommodate the currently arriving
	virtual network request (VNR) while preserving substrate resources for future requests.
	Conventional VNE criteria evaluate candidate suitability, embedding cost, or load balance.
	Existing fragmentation-aware metrics characterize resource imbalance, domain-specific fragments,
	or aggregated neighborhood states. However, these criteria do not isolate the loss of
	residual-resource connectivity caused by an embedding from the direct effect of resource
	consumption. This paper proposes an External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric.
	It represents residual-resource usability by combining the residual resource amount with a local
	normalized boundary capacity. The metric is specialized for substrate nodes and links according
	to the resource combinations involved in node placement and virtual-link routing. We then
	decompose the change in fragmentation into direct resource consumption and degradation of the
	connectivity of the remaining resources. Only the latter is retained as the induced fragmentation
	cost. Based on this cost, we formulate the online embedding problem and propose \ecfvne{}, a
	greedy algorithm. For each virtual node, \ecfvne{} evaluates every feasible substrate-node
	placement together with minimum-hop routes for its pending virtual links. All tentative
	embeddings of a VNR are evaluated relative to the same pre-request substrate state. We conduct
	experiments on an Internet Topology Zoo ISP network and on FatTree and BCube data center networks.
	\ecfvne{} achieves the highest acceptance ratio and time-average revenue among the compared
	fragmentation-aware and load-balancing baselines. Across the evaluated settings, it achieves up
	to $2.1\times$ and $2.6\times$ the acceptance ratio of the fragmentation-aware and load-balancing
	baselines, respectively.
	
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern network infrastructures must serve diverse tenants and applications simultaneously. These
services range from latency-sensitive traffic to bandwidth-intensive data transfers. Network
virtualization supports such heterogeneous requirements by abstracting physical infrastructure into
virtual resources. Each service is commonly represented as a virtual network request (VNR) with
node and link demands. Mapping these demands onto a substrate network is known as virtual network
embedding (VNE)~\cite{satpathy2025virtual}.

In practical deployments, VNRs arrive and depart over time. Embedding decisions must therefore be
made online under changing resource availability. The decision version of VNE is NP-complete, and
its optimization variants are NP-hard~\cite{rost2020hardness}. Practical methods consequently rely
on heuristics rather than globally optimal solutions. Each feasible embedding also changes the
substrate state available to later requests. Repeated allocation and release can leave resources in
a fragmented state even when sufficient aggregate capacity remains~\cite{fischer2013virtual}. A
locally feasible decision may therefore increase the rejection probability of future requests.

Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_figures} illustrates the resource fragmentation problem. Fig.~\ref{fig:vn}
shows a newly arriving VNR, while Fig.~\ref{fig:sn} represents the residual-resource state of the
substrate network. The substrate may have enough aggregate capacity for the request. Nevertheless,
the request can be rejected if the remaining CPU and bandwidth cannot be combined to satisfy its
node and link demands. The usefulness of a residual resource thus depends on both its amount and
its ability to connect with other resources.

Existing VNE criteria mainly evaluate candidate-node suitability, current embedding cost, or load
balance. Fragmentation-aware methods also consider resource imbalance or surrounding residual
resources. However, two issues remain. First, these quantities do not directly represent the
connectivity through which a residual resource can participate in a future VNR embedding. Second,
an absolute post-embedding fragmentation value can mix direct resource consumption with degradation
of the resources that remain.

We address the first issue through external connectivity. It is defined as the residual capacity of
a local resource boundary normalized by its initial capacity. For a substrate node, this boundary
consists of its incident links. It represents the bandwidth through which the residual CPU can
connect to other substrate resources. For a substrate link, connectivity is evaluated through the
external links at its endpoints. It represents the extent to which the link can participate in a
larger substrate path. We combine external connectivity with the residual resource amount to define
the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric. Node and link fragmentation then
share the common form $F=R(1-\mathrm{Conn})$.

We address the second issue by decomposing the change in fragmentation caused by an embedding. The
change contains direct resource consumption and degradation of residual-resource connectivity. We
retain only the latter as the \emph{induced fragmentation cost}. This cost does not decrease merely
because an already fragmented resource is consumed. It instead measures the loss of usability
imposed on the resources that remain.

Based on this cost, we formulate the online VNE problem and propose \ecfvne{}, a greedy embedding
algorithm. The algorithm processes virtual nodes in breadth-first order. For each virtual node, it
evaluates every CPU-feasible substrate node together with minimum-hop routes for the pending virtual
links. The resulting tentative embeddings are ranked by their induced fragmentation cost. All
candidates are evaluated relative to the substrate state immediately before the current VNR
arrived. This fixed reference permits consistent comparison across different stages of the same
request. The metric uses only local residual-bandwidth information from links adjacent to a node or
to the endpoints of a link.

The main contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
	\item We introduce the \ecf{} metric to represent the usability of residual node and link
	resources through normalized local boundary connectivity. The metric provides a unified form for
	node and link fragmentation. Its external-connectivity term is monotone under embedding.

	\item We decompose the change in fragmentation into direct resource consumption and degradation
	of residual-resource connectivity. Retaining only the latter yields a non-negative induced
	fragmentation cost. The cost does not reward an embedding merely for consuming an already
	fragmented resource.

	\item We formulate online VNE using the induced fragmentation cost and develop the greedy
	\ecfvne{} algorithm. Evaluation on an ISP topology and two data center topologies shows that
	\ecfvne{} improves the acceptance ratio and time-average revenue over the compared
	fragmentation-aware and load-balancing baselines across the tested arrival rates.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Resource- and Topology-Aware VNE}

Many VNE heuristics rank candidate substrate nodes using resource availability and topological
importance. PageRank-inspired methods combine resource and topology information to estimate
node-embedding potential. They subsequently route virtual links over shortest paths
~\cite{cao2017novel,hashmi2020vne}. Other methods use betweenness or closeness centrality, node
degree, link strength, or combinations of multiple topological attributes
~\cite{ding2015virtual,guler2016closeness,feng2014topology}. Multi-criteria approaches combine
heterogeneous node attributes into a unified ordering. NORD uses entropy-derived weights and
TOPSIS to rank virtual machines and substrate servers~\cite{tg2023nord}. ELECTRE-VNE instead uses a
simplified ELECTRE procedure to combine multiple node-ranking metrics~\cite{zhang2018virtual}.

Another line of work focuses on the interaction between node placement and virtual-link routing.
Independently selected node mappings can result in infeasible or unnecessarily costly link mappings.
Coordinated approaches therefore couple the two operations through joint optimization or
coordinated stages~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard,lu2023performance,nguyen2022toward}. Other VNE
formulations consider congestion and energy consumption~\cite{pham2019congestion}. Link-resource
and path characteristics are emphasized in~\cite{zheng2017link}. Latency and heterogeneous
resource loads are considered in~\cite{yan2019latency}.

Resource-distribution-aware methods address excessive concentration or imbalance of substrate
utilization. GRC-VNE evaluates node-embedding potential using resource information from the entire
substrate network~\cite{gong2014toward}. VHub and MLBVNE explicitly incorporate node or link load
balancing into their objectives~\cite{shanbhag2015vhub,li2022load}. These approaches determine where
and how to construct a feasible and efficient embedding. Their criteria mainly describe candidate
suitability, current embedding cost, or utilization balance. They do not explicitly measure the
connectivity loss induced in the residual substrate state. In particular, balanced utilization does
not guarantee that the remaining CPU and bandwidth can be combined for a future VNR.

\subsection{Fragmentation-Aware VNE}

Fragmentation-aware VNE addresses resources that remain available but become difficult to use. Guan
et al.~\cite{guan2023multidimensional} consider multidimensional resource fragmentation in MEC
systems interconnected by optical networks. They characterize physical-node fragmentation through
imbalance between computing and wireless resources. Physical-link fragmentation is represented by
unusable optical-spectrum fragments. Their bilevel formulation captures the dependency between node
embedding and path-and-spectrum allocation. This model is tailored to heterogeneous MEC resources
and optical-spectrum continuity. It therefore differs from the CPU--bandwidth connectivity studied
in this work.

Lu and Zhang~\cite{lu2022resource} introduce the resource fragmentation degree (RFD). RFD evaluates
the availability of a substrate resource using the states of surrounding resources. Node RFD
aggregates residual resources of nodes and paths within a hop-bounded neighborhood. Link RFD uses
adjacent links and their shared substrate nodes. Their VNE-RFD algorithm combines embedding-resource
cost with the residual-resource-weighted RFD of the post-embedding substrate state. RFD is the
closest formulation to the present work. Both approaches treat resource usability as dependent on
surrounding resources.

The proposed \ecf{} metric differs from these formulations along two dimensions. First, it
represents resource relationships through normalized local boundary capacity. For a substrate node,
the boundary consists of its incident links. For a substrate link, connectivity is evaluated through
the external links at its endpoints. This construction gives node and link fragmentation the common
form $F=R(1-\mathrm{Conn})$. It also keeps the evaluation local for online embedding.

Second, the proposed objective does not minimize an absolute post-embedding fragmentation value.
Such a value includes the direct reduction caused by consuming a resource. It can therefore decrease
when an already fragmented resource is consumed, even if no remaining resource becomes more usable.
We instead isolate the degradation in the connectivity of the resources that remain. This induced
fragmentation cost evaluates the loss of future resource combinability caused by an embedding. It
does not evaluate only the amount, balance, or absolute fragmentation of the remaining resources.

We model the substrate network as a weighted undirected graph $G^S=(N^S,L^S)$. The sets $N^S$ and
$L^S$ contain the substrate nodes and links, respectively. Table~\ref{tab:notation} summarizes the
main notation. For each substrate node $v\in N^S$, $c_v$ and $r_v$ denote its initial and residual
CPU capacities. For each substrate link $e=\{u,v\}\in L^S$, $c_e$ and $r_e$ denote its initial and
residual bandwidth capacities. Each undirected link $\{u,v\}$ is represented by two directed arcs,
$(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$. The set $A^S$ contains all such arcs.

We represent a VNR as a connected weighted undirected graph $G^V=(N^V,L^V)$. The sets $N^V$ and
$L^V$ contain the virtual nodes and links, respectively. The CPU demand of virtual node $n\in N^V$
is denoted by $d_n$. The bandwidth demand of virtual link $l=\{n,m\}\in L^V$ is denoted by $d_l$.

The node mapping is $M_N:N^V\rightarrow N^S$. Each virtual node is mapped to one substrate node.
Distinct virtual nodes of the same VNR are mapped to distinct substrate nodes. The binary variable
$x_{nv}$ equals one when virtual node $n$ is mapped to substrate node $v$.

At the formulation level, link mapping is represented by non-negative flow variables
$f_{uv}^l$. The variable $f_{uv}^l$ is the portion of demand $d_l$ routed through substrate arc
$(u,v)\in A^S$. These variables permit the demand of a virtual link to be split across multiple
substrate paths. A complete embedding is denoted by $M=(M_N,f)$. The residual node and link
resources after applying $M$ are
\begin{equation}
	r_v(M)
	=
	r_v-\sum_{n\in N^V}d_nx_{nv},
	\qquad \forall v\in N^S,
	\label{eq:res_node}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	r_{\{u,v\}}(M)
	=
	r_{\{u,v\}}
	-
	\sum_{l\in L^V}
	\left(
	f_{uv}^l+f_{vu}^l
	\right),
	\qquad
	\forall\{u,v\}\in L^S.
	\label{eq:res_link}
\end{equation}
The pre- and post-embedding residual substrate states given by \eqref{eq:res_node} and
\eqref{eq:res_link} serve as the basis for computing
the \ecf{} metric and the induced fragmentation cost in the following sections.

\section{External-Connectivity-Based Fragmentation Metric}
\label{sec:metric_definition_of_resource_fragmentation}

We now define the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf) metric, which combines residual
amount and external connectivity to represent the future usability of a residual resource. The
\ecf{} metric uses a common base form for nodes and links, but specializes to reflect the different
ways in which each resource combines with other resources in VNE.

\subsection{Base Definition and External Connectivity}
In VNE, the usability of a residual resource depends not only on its amount but also on its
combinability with surrounding resources. Let $R$ denote the residual amount and $\mathrm{Conn}$ the
normalized external connectivity; we view $R\cdot\mathrm{Conn}$ as the effectively usable portion of
the residual resource that can combine with external resources. We accordingly define the
fragmentation score as
\begin{equation}
	F=R(1-\mathrm{Conn}).
	\label{eq:base_frag}
\end{equation}
When $\mathrm{Conn}$ approaches one, the residual resource can be combined with external resources
and fragmentation is small. For the same residual amount, the fragmentation score grows as
external connectivity decreases.

To make external connectivity concrete, we consider any $\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq N^S$ and let
$\bar A=N^S\setminus A$. We define the boundary link set that connects the region $A$ with its
exterior $\bar A$ as
\begin{equation}
	\delta(A)
	=
	\{\,e=\{u,v\}\in L^S
	\mid
	u\in A,\ v\in\bar A\,\}.
	\label{eq:boundary}
\end{equation}
The initial and residual boundary capacities are $\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}c_e$ and
$\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}r_e$, respectively. The normalized external connectivity is
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Conn}(A)
	=
	\begin{cases}
		\dfrac{\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}r_e}{\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}c_e},
		  & \sum_{e\in\delta(A)}c_e>0, \\[1mm]
		0,
		  & \sum_{e\in\delta(A)}c_e=0. 
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:conn}
\end{equation}
Any bandwidth exchanged between $A$ and $\bar A$ must traverse $\delta(A)$. Its residual capacity
is therefore the total bandwidth through which region $A$ can connect with its exterior.
$\mathrm{Conn}(A)$ expresses this capacity as a fraction of the initial boundary capacity.

The connectivity term has two properties. First, $\mathrm{Conn}(A)$ bounds the external usability
of $A$. If $\mathrm{Conn}(A)=0$, no additional bandwidth can cross the boundary. Resources inside
$A$ then cannot be combined with external resources. Second, $\mathrm{Conn}$ is monotone under
embedding. An embedding only removes bandwidth from $\delta(A)$, and thus
$\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}r_e(M)\le\sum_{e\in\delta(A)}r_e$ and therefore
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Conn}(A;M)\le\mathrm{Conn}(A),
	\qquad
	\forall\,\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq N^S,
	\label{eq:monotone}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{Conn}(A;M)$ denotes the external connectivity evaluated in the residual state after
embedding $M$. These two properties keep the metric
interpretable as a resource-usability bound whose external connectivity cannot improve merely as a
byproduct of embedding.

The remainder of this section instantiates $A$ as a single substrate node or the endpoints of a
single substrate link. These regions correspond to the local resource combinations considered for
node embedding and virtual-link routing. This choice keeps $\mathrm{Conn}$ local to the adjacent
links of a resource. The same definition also applies to larger regions. Normalization by the
initial boundary capacity removes the absolute bandwidth scale. Node and link fragmentation can
therefore be expressed in a common form.

\subsection{Node Fragmentation}
For a substrate node $v\in N^S$, we set $A=\{v\}$ in \eqref{eq:conn}, so that the external
connectivity of the node is given by
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Conn}_N(v)
	=
	\begin{cases}
		\dfrac{\sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}r_e}
		{\sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}c_e},
		  & \sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}c_e>0, \\[1mm]
		0,
		  & \text{otherwise.}              
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:conn_n}
\end{equation}
In VNE, the residual CPU of a substrate node must combine with the bandwidth of its adjacent links
to reach other substrate resources; therefore, a reduction in adjacent bandwidth lowers the future
usability of that CPU. Accordingly, we define the node connectivity-deficiency factor and the node
fragmentation score as
\begin{equation}
	I_N(v)=1-\mathrm{Conn}_N(v),
	\qquad
	F_N(v)=r_v I_N(v).
	\label{eq:node_frag}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Link Fragmentation}

For a substrate link $e=\{u,v\}\in L^S$, its residual bandwidth is useful
for future virtual-link embeddings only when the link can be extended
through residual bandwidth resources around its endpoints. We therefore
evaluate the external connectivity of $e$ separately at $u$ and $v$, while
excluding $e$ itself from the neighboring-link set of each endpoint.

For an endpoint $z\in\{u,v\}$, the external-link set is
$\delta(\{z\})\setminus\{e\}$. It contains the substrate links incident to $z$, excluding $e$.
When these links have positive total initial capacity, their residual-to-initial capacity ratio
defines the endpoint connectivity of $z$ with respect to $e$:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Conn}_z(e)
	=
	\frac{\sum_{e'\in\delta(\{z\})\setminus\{e\}}r_{e'}}
	{\sum_{e'\in\delta(\{z\})\setminus\{e\}}c_{e'}},
	\qquad
	\sum_{e'\in\delta(\{z\})\setminus\{e\}}c_{e'}>0.
	\label{eq:conn_endpoint}
\end{equation}
If the total initial capacity is zero, endpoint $z$ has no external link through which $e$ can be
extended. This endpoint is excluded from the link-connectivity comparison.

Whenever an endpoint $z \in \{u, v\}$ has a positive total initial capacity
among its external links, it is included in the comparison. We collect such
endpoints as
\begin{equation}
	\mathcal{Z}_e
	=
	\left\{\, z \in \{u,v\} \;\middle|\;
	\sum_{e' \in \delta(\{z\}) \setminus \{e\}} c_{e'} > 0 \,\right\}.
	\label{eq:zset}
\end{equation}
The external connectivity of $e$ is then determined by the minimum
endpoint connectivity among the endpoints in $\mathcal{Z}_e$:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Conn}_L(e) =
	\begin{cases}
		\displaystyle \min_{z \in \mathcal{Z}_e} \quad \mathrm{Conn}_z(e), & \text{if } \mathcal{Z}_e \neq \emptyset, \\[1.5ex]
		0,                                                                 & \text{otherwise.}                        
	\end{cases}
	\label{eq:conn_l}
\end{equation}
Accordingly, three cases arise naturally. If both endpoints have external link resources, the
smaller of their two connectivity values is used. If only one endpoint has external link
resources, the connectivity of that endpoint is used. If neither endpoint has an external link
resource, the link has no external extension opportunity, and its external connectivity is zero.

The link connectivity-deficiency factor and link fragmentation score are then given by
\begin{equation}
	I_L(e)=1-\mathrm{Conn}_L(e),
	\qquad
	F_L(e)=r_e I_L(e).
	\label{eq:link_frag}
\end{equation}

This definition applies a bottleneck principle only when a comparison is
meaningful. It does not penalize a link merely because one endpoint is a
leaf-like endpoint with no other incident substrate link. However, when
both endpoints lack external neighboring resources, the residual bandwidth
of the link is fully isolated; hence $\mathrm{Conn}_L(e)=0$ and
$I_L(e)=1$.

\subsection{Induced Fragmentation Cost}

The difference between the pre- and post-embedding fragmentation scores
contains both the direct effect of resource consumption and the effect of
connectivity degradation on the resources that remain. For a general
fragmentation score $F=RI$, the change caused by an embedding $M$ can be
decomposed as
\begin{equation}
	F(M)-F
	=
	\bigl(R(M)-R\bigr)I
	+
	R(M)\bigl(I(M)-I\bigr).
	\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
The first term represents direct resource consumption. The second term represents the loss of
usability caused by degraded residual-resource connectivity. The induced fragmentation cost retains
only the second term.

For each arriving VNR, $S_0$ denotes the residual state before processing begins. State $S(M)$
denotes the residual state after a partial or complete embedding $M$. We apply the second term of
\eqref{eq:decomp} to every substrate node and link. The induced node and link fragmentation are

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\Delta F_N(M) = \sum_{v\in N^S} & r_v(S(M))                                             \\
		                                & \quad \times \left( I_N(v;S(M)) - I_N(v;S_0) \right), 
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:delta_fn}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\Delta F_L(M) = \sum_{e\in L^S} & r_e(S(M))                                             \\
		                                & \quad \times \left( I_L(e;S(M)) - I_L(e;S_0) \right), 
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:delta_fl}
\end{equation}
where $I_N(\cdot;S)$ and $I_L(\cdot;S)$ denote the connectivity-deficiency factors
\eqref{eq:node_frag} and \eqref{eq:link_frag} evaluated in state $S$.

Both quantities are non-negative. An embedding consumes resources and removes bandwidth from the
associated boundaries. By \eqref{eq:monotone}, the external connectivity of a node or link cannot
increase. Thus, $I_N(v;S(M))-I_N(v;S_0)\ge0$ for every $v\in N^S$. Likewise,
$I_L(e;S(M))-I_L(e;S_0)\ge0$ for every $e\in L^S$. The residual-resource factors $r_v(S(M))$ and
$r_e(S(M))$ are also non-negative for every feasible embedding.

The residual-resource factors weight degradation according to the resources that remain after the
embedding. The term $\Delta F_N$ penalizes bandwidth consumption adjacent to nodes with large
residual CPU. The term $\Delta F_L$ penalizes bandwidth consumption adjacent to links with large
residual bandwidth. A low induced cost therefore favors embeddings that leave residual resources
well connected.


\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

The objective is to embed the current VNR while preserving the future usability of residual
substrate resources. State $S_0$ is the residual substrate state immediately before the request is
processed. State $S(M)$ is the residual state after a partial or complete embedding $M$. The
reference state $S_0$ remains fixed while the current request is processed. The variables $x_{nv}$
and $f_{uv}^l$ represent node placement and virtual-link flow, respectively.

\textbf{Objective:}

Feasible embeddings are ranked by the induced fragmentation cost imposed on the substrate network.
The objective function is
$J_{\mathrm{ECF}}(M) = \beta\,\Delta F_L(M) + \gamma\,\Delta F_N(M)$.
The terms $\Delta F_L(M)$ and $\Delta F_N(M)$ are defined in \eqref{eq:delta_fl} and
\eqref{eq:delta_fn}. Both are evaluated relative to $S_0$. The non-negative weights $\beta$ and
$\gamma$ control the link and node fragmentation terms, respectively. The online VNE objective is
\begin{equation}
	\underset{M}{\text{minimize}}\quad J_{\mathrm{ECF}}(M).
	\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
The proposed \ecfvne{} algorithm uses this objective to rank unsplittable tentative embeddings
during its greedy search.

\textbf{Constraints:}

\textbf{Node assignment constraints:}
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{v\in N^S}x_{nv}=1,
	\qquad
	\forall n\in N^V,
	\label{eq:node_assign}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{n\in N^V}x_{nv}\le1,
	\qquad
	\forall v\in N^S.
	\label{eq:node_unique}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Capacity constraints:}
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{n\in N^V}d_nx_{nv}
	\le r_v,
	\qquad
	\forall v\in N^S,
	\label{eq:cpu_cap}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\sum_{l\in L^V}
	\left(
	f_{uv}^l+f_{vu}^l
	\right)
	\le r_{\{u,v\}},
	\qquad
	\forall\{u,v\}\in L^S.
	\label{eq:bw_cap}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Flow-conservation constraints:}
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		\sum_{(v,w)\in A^S}f_{vw}^l
		-
		\sum_{(w,v)\in A^S}f_{wv}^l
		  & =                      
		d_l\left(x_{nv}-x_{mv}\right),
		\\
		  & \forall v\in N^S,\quad 
		\forall l=\{n,m\}\in L^V.
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:flow_cons}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Remarks:}
\begin{itemize}
	\item The objective~\eqref{eq:objective} is non-negative, since both \eqref{eq:delta_fl} and
	      \eqref{eq:delta_fn} are non-negative by the monotonicity property~\eqref{eq:monotone}. The two
	      terms are not normalized by substrate-wide resource capacities; therefore, the weights $\beta$
	      and $\gamma$ also account for possible differences in the numerical scales of CPU and bandwidth
	      resources.
	\item Constraint sets~\eqref{eq:node_assign} and~\eqref{eq:node_unique} enforce that each virtual
	      node is mapped to exactly one substrate node and that distinct virtual nodes of the same VNR are
	      not mapped to the same substrate node.
	\item Constraint~\eqref{eq:cpu_cap} ensures that the CPU demand allocated to a substrate node
	      does not exceed its current residual CPU capacity.
	\item Constraint~\eqref{eq:bw_cap} ensures that the total bandwidth allocated on an undirected
	      substrate link, summed over both directions, does not exceed its residual bandwidth.
	\item Constraint~\eqref{eq:flow_cons} enforces flow conservation for each virtual link
	      $l=\{n,m\}\in L^V$. The net flow is zero at every other substrate node. The substrate nodes
	      hosting $n$ and $m$ act as the source and destination.
\end{itemize}

\section{\ecfvne{} Embedding Algorithm}
\label{sec:embedding_algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{\ecfvne{} Main Greedy Embedding Algorithm}
	\label{alg:ecf_main}
	\footnotesize
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE \textbf{Input:} substrate network $G^S$, VNR $G^V$, weights
		$\beta$ and $\gamma$
		\STATE \textbf{Output:} embedding $M$ or rejection
		\STATE $S_0\leftarrow$ current residual state of $G^S$
		\STATE Select root $r\in N^V$ by lexicographically maximizing
		$\left(\deg(n),d_n,\sum_{l\in L^V:n\in l}d_l\right)$
		\STATE Obtain BFS processing order $\pi$ from $r$
		\STATE $M\leftarrow\emptyset$
		\STATE $v_r\leftarrow$\textsc{RootPlacement}$(G^S,r)$
		\IF{$v_r=\emptyset$}
		\STATE \textbf{return} CPU rejection
		\ENDIF
		\STATE $M\leftarrow M\cup\{r\mapsto v_r\}$
		
		\FOR{each virtual node $n\in\pi\setminus\{r\}$}
		\STATE $\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\emptyset$
		\STATE $\mathit{hasCpuCandidate}\leftarrow\textbf{false}$
		
		\FOR{each substrate node $v\in N^S$}
		\IF{$v$ is used by $M_N$ \OR $r_v<d_n$}
		\STATE \textbf{continue}
		\ENDIF
		
		\STATE $\mathit{hasCpuCandidate}\leftarrow\textbf{true}$
		\STATE $\widehat{M}\leftarrow M\cup\{n\mapsto v\}$
		\STATE $\mathit{feasible}\leftarrow\textbf{true}$
		\STATE $\mathcal{L}_n\leftarrow
		\{l=\{n,m\}\in L^V:m\text{ is mapped in }M\}$
		
		\FOR{each $l=\{n,m\}\in\mathcal{L}_n$ in increasing link-index order}
		\STATE $p\leftarrow$\textsc{MinimumHopPath}
		$(G^S,v,M_N(m),d_l)$
		\IF{$p=\emptyset$}
		\STATE $\mathit{feasible}\leftarrow\textbf{false}$
		\STATE \textbf{break}
		\ENDIF
		\STATE $\widehat{M}\leftarrow\widehat{M}\cup\{l\mapsto p\}$
		\ENDFOR
		
		\IF{$\mathit{feasible}$}
		\STATE $\mathcal{C}\leftarrow\mathcal{C}\cup
		\{(\widehat{M},J_{\mathrm{ECF}}(\widehat{M}))\}$
		\ENDIF
		\ENDFOR
		
		\IF{$\mathcal{C}=\emptyset$}
		\IF{\textbf{not} $\mathit{hasCpuCandidate}$}
		\STATE \textbf{return} CPU rejection
		\ELSE
		\STATE \textbf{return} routing rejection
		\ENDIF
		\ENDIF
		
		\STATE $(M,\cdot)\leftarrow
		\arg\min_{(\widehat{M},J)\in\mathcal{C}} J$
		\ENDFOR
		\STATE \textbf{return} $M$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}


\begin{algorithm}[!t]
	\caption{RootPlacement}
	\label{alg:root_placement}
	\footnotesize
	\begin{algorithmic}[1]
		\STATE \textbf{Input:} substrate network $G^S$, root virtual node $r$
		\STATE \textbf{Output:} substrate node $v_r$ or $\emptyset$
		\STATE $v_r\leftarrow\emptyset$
		\STATE $I^*\leftarrow+\infty$
		\STATE $R^*\leftarrow-1$
		
		\FOR{each substrate node $v\in N^S$}
		\IF{$r_v<d_r$}
		\STATE \textbf{continue}
		\ENDIF
		
		\STATE $I\leftarrow I_N(v)$
		\IF{$v_r=\emptyset$ \OR $I<I^*$
			\OR $(I=I^* \AND r_v>R^*)$
			\OR $(I=I^* \AND r_v=R^* \AND v<v_r)$}
		\STATE $v_r\leftarrow v$
		\STATE $I^*\leftarrow I$
		\STATE $R^*\leftarrow r_v$
		\ENDIF
		\ENDFOR
		
		\STATE \textbf{return} $v_r$
	\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecf_main} summarizes the sequential embedding procedure of \ecfvne{}. The flow
formulation permits split routing, whereas \ecfvne{} assigns each virtual link to one substrate
path. For that virtual link, a selected path sets $f_{uv}^l=d_l$ on its arcs and zero on all other
arcs. The algorithm stores the pre-request state as $S_0$. It evaluates each feasible partial embedding using
$J_{\mathrm{ECF}}(M)$ in \eqref{eq:objective}. Candidates from different stages of the same VNR are
therefore evaluated relative to one fixed state. Numerical and lexicographical ties are broken by
the smaller index of the corresponding virtual or substrate entity.

The algorithm begins by selecting a root virtual node, namely the one that lexicographically
maximizes the tuple $\bigl(\deg(n),\,d_n,\,\sum_{l\in L^V:n\in l}d_l\bigr)$. This choice
prioritizes virtual nodes that have more incident constraints and larger resource requirements.
Starting from the selected root, the remaining virtual nodes are processed in breadth-first
order.

The root substrate node is selected by \textsc{RootPlacement} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:root_placement}),
which considers substrate nodes with sufficient residual CPU. It selects the node with the minimum
node connectivity-deficiency factor. Ties are resolved by larger residual CPU capacity and then by
smaller substrate-node index.

After root placement, \ecfvne{} processes each remaining virtual node $n$.
It considers every substrate node $v$ that has sufficient residual CPU and
has not already been assigned to another virtual node of the same VNR. For
each such candidate, the algorithm makes a temporary copy of the current
partial embedding, maps $n$ to $v$, and identifies the virtual links from
$n$ to already mapped virtual neighbors. Those pending virtual links are
processed in increasing virtual-link index order.

For a pending virtual link $l=\{n,m\}$, the algorithm uses breadth-first
search to find a single minimum-hop path from $v$ to $M_N(m)$. During the
search, a substrate link is traversable only if its current residual
bandwidth satisfies the demand of $l$. When several minimum-hop paths are
available, the search resolves the choice by the index-based tie-breaking rule stated above.
The demand of $l$ is allocated immediately after the path is found. A later pending link therefore
observes the resource consumption of earlier links in the same tentative candidate.

A candidate substrate node is infeasible if any pending virtual link cannot
be routed. Otherwise, its resulting tentative embedding is evaluated by
$J_{\mathrm{ECF}}$, which measures the induced node and link fragmentation
relative to $S_0$. Among all feasible candidates, the algorithm commits the
one with the minimum objective value. The algorithm is greedy: after a candidate is
committed, earlier node and link decisions are not revisited. A VNR is
rejected due to CPU infeasibility when no unused substrate node can satisfy
the current virtual-node CPU demand. It is rejected due to routing
infeasibility when CPU-feasible candidates exist but none can route all of
their required pending virtual links.

\subsection{Complexity Analysis}

Let $\Delta_S$ denote the maximum degree of a substrate node. Because the algorithm maps distinct
virtual nodes of the same VNR to distinct substrate nodes, a feasible request always satisfies
$|N^V|\le|N^S|$.

For each virtual node, \ecfvne{} examines at most $|N^S|$ substrate-node candidates. The aggregate
candidate-enumeration cost is $O(|N^S||N^V|)$. Each virtual link is routed when its second endpoint
is mapped. For each tested candidate, a minimum-hop breadth-first search costs
$O(|N^S|+|L^S|)$. The aggregate path-search cost is therefore
$O\bigl(|N^S||L^V|(|N^S|+|L^S|)\bigr)$.

For one ECF objective evaluation, all node connectivity-deficiency values are obtained by scanning
the substrate links once. This step costs $O(|L^S|)$. For link connectivity deficiency, the
external links incident to both endpoints of every substrate link are examined. The total cost is
$O\bigl(\sum_{v\in N^S}\deg(v)^2\bigr)=O(|L^S|\Delta_S)$. Thus, one evaluation of
$J_{\mathrm{ECF}}$ costs $O(|L^S|\Delta_S)$. At most $O(|N^S||N^V|)$ candidate embeddings are
evaluated. The total objective-evaluation cost is
$O\bigl(|N^S||N^V||L^S|\Delta_S\bigr)$.

The overall worst-case running time is therefore
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		O\Bigl(
		|N^S||N^V|+|N^S||L^V|\bigl( & |N^S|+|L^S|\bigr)        \\
		                            & +|N^S||N^V||L^S|\Delta_S 
		\Bigr).
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:complexity}
\end{equation}
This bound depends on the substrate topology through $|N^S|$, $|L^S|$, and $\Delta_S$. It also
depends on the request size through $|N^V|$ and $|L^V|$. The request-side quantities remain
explicit because the VNR size is independent of the substrate size. We consider two representative
topology regimes. For a sparse substrate, where $|L^S|=O(|N^S|)$ and $\Delta_S=O(1)$, the
running time reduces to $T_{\mathrm{sparse}}=O\bigl(|N^S|^2|N^V|+|N^S|^2|L^V|\bigr)$. For a dense
substrate, where $|L^S|=O(|N^S|^2)$ and $\Delta_S=O(|N^S|)$, it reduces to
$T_{\mathrm{dense}}=O\bigl(|N^S|^4|N^V|+|N^S|^3|L^V|\bigr)$.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance_evaluation}

This section evaluates \ecfvne{} against representative fragmentation-aware and load-balancing-based
VNE algorithms on an ISP topology and two data center network (DCN) topologies. We first describe
the simulation setup, the compared algorithms, the network settings, and the performance metrics,
and then present the results under a fixed VNR arrival rate followed by the results under
increasing arrival rates.

\subsection{Evaluation Setup}

All algorithms are implemented and evaluated within the VIRNE simulation
environment~\cite{wang_virne}. We consider a dynamic online VNE scenario in which VNRs arrive
according to a Poisson process and stay in the substrate network for an exponentially distributed
lifetime, after which their resources are released. Each substrate network--algorithm pair is
evaluated over at least 10 independent simulation runs with different random seeds, and the
reported values are averaged over these runs. To capture steady-state behavior, an initial warm-up
period equal to ten times the mean VNR lifetime is excluded from the measurement, and each run is
then measured over 50{,}000 time units.

\subsection{Compared Algorithms}

We compare \ecfvne{} with two groups of baselines. The first group consists of the
resource-fragmentation-based algorithms VNE-RFD-B and VNE-RFD-D~\cite{lu2022resource}, which embed
each VNR so as to minimize the resource fragmentation degree (RFD) reviewed in
Section~\ref{sec:related_work}; the two variants differ only in whether the virtual nodes following
the root are ordered by breadth-first or depth-first search. The
second group consists of the load-balancing-based algorithms D-ViNE-LB and
R-ViNE-LB~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard}, which coordinate node and link mapping through a relaxed
mixed-integer program and select substrate nodes by deterministic and randomized rounding,
respectively. For VNE-RFD-B and VNE-RFD-D, the backtracking bound, the number of candidate paths,
and the hop bound of the fragmentation neighborhood are set to $K=10$, and $\tau=3$
for computational tractability. Placement-location constraints are disabled for all algorithms so
that every substrate node with sufficient CPU is a valid candidate. Unless otherwise specified, the
remaining algorithm-specific parameters follow the settings reported in the original papers. We set $\beta=\gamma=1$ throughout the experiments to use equal weighting without topology-specific parameter tuning; the same values are used for all substrate topologies and offered loads.

VNE-RFD-B shares its overall structure with \ecfvne{}: the root virtual node is selected by degree
and resource demand, the remaining virtual nodes are processed in breadth-first order, and every
CPU-feasible substrate node is evaluated for each virtual node. The two algorithms differ in the
fragmentation metric that drives node selection, in the path search ($K$ candidate minimum-hop paths VS. a single one), in the root placement rule, and in the presence of backtracking, which
\ecfvne{} does not use. The comparison with VNE-RFD-B therefore primarily reflects the difference
between the two fragmentation metrics.

\subsection{Network Settings}

\subsubsection{Substrate Networks}

We use three substrate networks that represent two distinct deployment environments. As an ISP
network, we adopt the Columbus topology from the Internet Topology Zoo~\cite{knight2011internet},
which consists of 70 nodes and 85 links. Its node CPU capacities and link bandwidth capacities are
drawn uniformly from $U[50,100]$ and $U[250,500]$, respectively, so that the substrate is
heterogeneous both in topology and in capacity.

As DCNs, we adopt a FatTree topology (FT6) with 64 hosts and 45 switches and a BCube topology,
BCube$(2,4)$, with 64 hosts and 48 switches. In both DCNs, each host has a CPU capacity of 100 and
every link has a bandwidth capacity of 100. Switches have zero CPU capacity; virtual nodes can be
mapped only onto hosts, and switches are used solely for forwarding virtual links~\cite{blenk2018neurovine,haeri2016virtual}. The two DCNs
therefore share the same number of hosts and the same per-element capacities but differ in how
hosts attach to the fabric: in FT6 each host is connected to the network through a single link,
whereas in BCube$(2,4)$ each host is connected to multiple switches at different levels.

\subsubsection{Virtual Network Requests}

Each VNR has a number of virtual nodes drawn from the discrete uniform distribution over $[2,10]$,
and its topology is a connected Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph with connection probability $p=0.4$.
Virtual node CPU demands and virtual link bandwidth demands are drawn from $U[1,20]$ and
$U[1,50]$, respectively. VNRs arrive according to a Poisson process with a rate of 40 arrivals per
1{,}000 time units unless otherwise specified, and their lifetimes are exponentially distributed
with a mean of 1{,}000 time units. The same VNR generation settings are used for all substrate
networks so that differences across networks are attributable to the substrate rather than to the
workload.

\subsection{Performance Metrics}

We evaluate the VNR acceptance ratio, average revenue rate, average embedding cost, and average
node and link utilization. These metrics follow the definitions used by the compared VNE
algorithms~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard,lu2022resource}. Let
$\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{arr}}(T)$ be the VNRs arriving during a measurement interval of length $T$,
and let $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{acc}}(T)\subseteq\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{arr}}(T)$ be the accepted VNRs.
The acceptance ratio is
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{AR}(T)
	=
	\frac{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{acc}}(T)|}
	{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{arr}}(T)|}.
	\label{eq:acceptance-ratio}
\end{equation}

For a VNR $q$ with $G_q^V=(N_q^V,L_q^V)$, its revenue is the sum of its requested CPU and
bandwidth resources:
\begin{equation}
	\mathrm{Rev}(q)
	=
	\sum_{n\in N_q^V}d_n
	+
	\sum_{l\in L_q^V}d_l.
	\label{eq:vnr-revenue}
\end{equation}
Let $M_q$ be the embedding of an accepted VNR $q$. We denote the flow of virtual link $l$ on
substrate arc $(u,v)$ under $M_q$ by $f_{uv}^{l}$. Its embedding cost is the total allocated CPU
and bandwidth over all substrate links:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
	\mathrm{Cost}(q,M_q)
	={}&\sum_{n\in N_q^V}d_n \\
	&+\sum_{\{u,v\}\in L^S}\sum_{l\in L_q^V}
	\left(f_{uv}^{l}+f_{vu}^{l}\right).
	\end{aligned}
	\label{eq:vnr-embedding-cost}
\end{equation}
For an unsplittable mapping, the link term in \eqref{eq:vnr-embedding-cost} equals
$\sum_{l\in L_q^V}d_l|P_q(l)|$, where $P_q(l)$ is the substrate path assigned to $l$.

The average revenue rate measures the accepted resource demand per unit time:
\begin{equation}
	\overline{\mathrm{Rev}}(T)
	=
	\frac{1}{T}
	\sum_{q\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{acc}}(T)}
	\mathrm{Rev}(q).
	\label{eq:average-revenue-rate}
\end{equation}
The average embedding cost per accepted VNR is
\begin{equation}
	\overline{\mathrm{Cost}}(T)
	=
	\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{acc}}(T)|}
	\sum_{q\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{acc}}(T)}
	\mathrm{Cost}(q,M_q).
	\label{eq:average-embedding-cost}
\end{equation}

Let $r_v(t)$ and $r_e(t)$ denote the residual CPU and bandwidth at time $t$, respectively. The
time-average node and link utilizations are
\begin{equation}
	\overline{U}_N(T)
	=
	\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T
	\frac{\sum_{v\in N^S}\left(c_v-r_v(t)\right)}
	{\sum_{v\in N^S}c_v}\,dt,
	\label{eq:average-node-utilization}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
	\overline{U}_L(T)
	=
	\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T
	\frac{\sum_{e\in L^S}\left(c_e-r_e(t)\right)}
	{\sum_{e\in L^S}c_e}\,dt.
	\label{eq:average-link-utilization}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Acceptance Ratio}

Fig.~\ref{fig:acceptance} shows the VNR acceptance ratio under an arrival rate of 40 VNRs per
1{,}000 time units. \ecfvne{} achieves the highest acceptance ratio on all three substrate
networks. On Columbus, \ecfvne{} accepts 93.2\% of the arriving VNRs, compared with 52.5\% and
51.9\% for VNE-RFD-B and VNE-RFD-D and 35.8\% for both D-ViNE-LB and R-ViNE-LB; that is, the acceptance
ratio is improved by a factor of about 1.8 over the fragmentation-based baselines and about 2.6
over the load-balancing baselines. On BCube$(2,4)$, \ecfvne{} accepts 68.8\% of the VNRs, whereas
the best baseline, R-ViNE-LB, accepts 46.7\%, and the RFD-based algorithms accept only about 34\%. On
FT6, all algorithms attain comparatively low acceptance ratios, and \ecfvne{} achieves 38.8\%
against 35.1\% for R-ViNE-LB and about 22\% for the RFD-based algorithms.

Two observations follow from these results. First, the relative ordering of the two baseline
groups reverses between the ISP network and the DCNs. On Columbus, the RFD-based algorithms clearly
outperform D-ViNE-LB and R-ViNE-LB, which is consistent with the premise that fragmentation awareness is
beneficial on heterogeneous topologies. On FT6 and BCube$(2,4)$, however, VNE-RFD-B and VNE-RFD-D
fall well below the load-balancing baselines, accepting only about 64\% and 75\% as many VNRs as
D-ViNE-LB, respectively. We attribute this degradation to the way RFD aggregates the residual
resources of a hop-bounded neighborhood. The link RFD weights each adjacent link by the residual
CPU ratio of the shared node, so every link incident to a zero-CPU switch is assigned the maximum
fragmentation degree irrespective of its residual bandwidth, and the node RFD of a host averages
over a neighborhood that consists mostly of such switches. On a regular topology with uniform
link capacities, the resulting values are nearly identical across hosts and provide little
guidance for node selection. In contrast, \ecfvne{} preserves its advantage on the DCNs
because the external connectivity of a host is evaluated directly through the residual bandwidth
of its attached links, which is exactly the resource that determines whether the host can still
support virtual nodes with incident virtual links. The proposed metric therefore remains
discriminative even when CPU capacities are uniform and the topology is symmetric.

Second, the magnitude of the gain depends on the structural bottleneck of the substrate. On FT6,
each host is attached to the fabric through a single link with a capacity of 100, so the aggregate
bandwidth of all virtual links incident to a virtual node must traverse that single link. Since a
virtual link demand can be as large as 50, this access link constrains the attainable acceptance
ratio of every algorithm, which explains the uniformly low values on FT6 and the smaller relative
improvement of \ecfvne{}. BCube$(2,4)$ provides each host with multiple attachment links, which
relaxes this bottleneck; accordingly, all algorithms achieve higher acceptance ratios and the
benefit of avoiding fragmentation of the host-side bandwidth becomes more pronounced, resulting in
a gain of about 22 percentage points over the best baseline.

Fig.~\ref{fig:revenue} presents the time-average revenue. The revenue results amplify the
acceptance results because \ecfvne{} not only accepts more VNRs but also sustains a larger number
of concurrently embedded VNRs in the substrate. On Columbus, \ecfvne{} generates about 2.7 times the
revenue of the RFD-based algorithms and about 4.6 times that of the ViNE algorithms. On
BCube$(2,4)$, the corresponding factors are about 3.2--3.3 and 1.8, and on FT6 they are about 2.3 and
1.06--1.09. The comparatively small revenue margin over R-ViNE-LB on FT6 is again a consequence of
the access-link bottleneck discussed above, which limits how much additional demand any algorithm
can accommodate.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cost} shows the average embedding cost per accepted VNR. Here the RFD-based
algorithms attain the lowest cost on all networks, \ecfvne{} incurs a moderately higher cost, and
D-ViNE-LB and R-ViNE-LB incur the highest cost. The cost of \ecfvne{} is 1.25 to 1.76 times that of
VNE-RFD-B but only 54\% to 68\% of that of D-ViNE-LB. The higher cost relative to the RFD-based
algorithms should be interpreted together with the acceptance ratio: \ecfvne{} accepts VNRs that
the RFD-based algorithms reject, and these additional VNRs are embedded in a substrate whose
residual capacity is more heavily utilized, so their virtual links are routed over somewhat longer
paths. Moreover, the objective of \ecfvne{} deliberately trades a small increase in the present
embedding cost for lower induced fragmentation whenever a slightly longer path leaves the remaining
resources better connected. Despite this, the increase in average embedding cost is accompanied by a
substantially larger time-average revenue, because \ecfvne{} accepts and
sustains more VNRs. Thus, the additional provisioning cost is offset by the
higher accommodated demand.

\subsubsection{Resource Utilization}

Fig.~\ref{fig:util} shows the average node and link utilization. \ecfvne{} achieves the highest
node utilization on all networks, about 2.1 to 2.7 times that of the RFD-based algorithms and 1.1
to 3.6 times that of the ViNE algorithms. Since node utilization is proportional to the CPU demand
of the VNRs that are simultaneously embedded, this result confirms that the higher acceptance ratio
of \ecfvne{} translates into a correspondingly larger amount of admitted demand rather than into
the admission of small requests only.

Link utilization reveals a more subtle difference between the two baseline groups. On Columbus,
\ecfvne{} also attains the highest link utilization. On FT6 and BCube$(2,4)$, however, D-ViNE-LB and
R-ViNE-LB reach link utilizations comparable to or higher than that of \ecfvne{} even though they
accept fewer VNRs and admit less CPU demand. The ratio of link utilization to node utilization is
about 3.2 to 3.9 for the ViNE algorithms but only 1.6 to 2.2 for \ecfvne{}, which means that the
ViNE algorithms consume roughly twice as much bandwidth per unit of admitted CPU demand. This is
the utilization-side counterpart of their high embedding cost: load balancing distributes the
flows of a VNR over longer paths, occupying bandwidth on many links without a corresponding
increase in accepted requests. The RFD-based algorithms exhibit the lowest utilization of both
resources, which reflects their conservative admission rather than efficient use of the substrate.
\ecfvne{} thus converts substrate capacity into accepted VNRs most efficiently, keeping the
consumption of bandwidth in proportion to the admitted demand while leaving the residual resources
in a combinable state.

To examine the robustness of the results with respect to the offered load, we vary the VNR arrival
rate from 40 to 80 arrivals per 1{,}000 time units while keeping all other settings fixed.
Fig.~\ref{fig:acceptance_arrival_rate} shows the resulting acceptance ratios. \ecfvne{} retains the
highest acceptance ratio at every arrival rate on every substrate network; no crossover with any
baseline is observed within the tested range.

The acceptance ratio of every algorithm decreases as the arrival rate increases, but the rate of
decrease differs markedly across algorithm groups. The load-balancing baselines degrade most
rapidly, losing 26\% to 31\% of their acceptance ratio between the lowest and highest arrival
rates on all three networks. The RFD-based algorithms degrade the least, by 6\% to 15\%, but from
an already low level; their acceptance is limited primarily by their conservative embedding
decisions rather than by the offered load, so additional load has a comparatively small effect.
\ecfvne{} lies in between, with a relative decrease of 15\% to 21\%. Because \ecfvne{} operates
much closer to the capacity of the substrate, it is naturally more exposed to the increased
contention, yet its acceptance ratio at the highest arrival rate remains above the acceptance
ratio of every baseline at the lowest arrival rate on Columbus and BCube$(2,4)$.

The evolution of the margin over the strongest baseline is also informative. On Columbus, the
absolute gap over VNE-RFD-B narrows from about 41 to 29 percentage points as the load increases,
while the acceptance ratio of \ecfvne{} remains 1.6 times that of VNE-RFD-B at the highest rate. On
BCube$(2,4)$, the gap over the ViNE algorithms is essentially constant at about 20 to 22 percentage
points, and the ratio increases from 1.47 to 1.58. On FT6, the margin widens with load: the gap
over R-ViNE-LB grows from about 4 to 9 percentage points and the ratio from 1.11 to 1.36, because the
ViNE algorithms lose acceptance quickly while \ecfvne{} degrades gracefully. This indicates that
the advantage of preserving the combinability of residual resources becomes more pronounced as the
substrate becomes more congested, since under heavier load a fragmented residual state is
increasingly likely to cause the rejection of otherwise feasible requests. Overall, the results
show that the improvement of \ecfvne{} is not specific to a particular operating point but
persists, and in the DCNs even strengthens, as the offered load increases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric for online
VNE. The metric evaluates residual resources using both their remaining capacity and their
connectivity to other resources. We also defined the induced fragmentation cost by separating
direct resource consumption from the connectivity degradation caused by an embedding. Based on
this cost, we developed \ecfvne{}, which evaluates feasible node placements using single
minimum-hop paths without backtracking.

The evaluation covered an ISP topology and two data center topologies. At the fixed arrival rate,
\ecfvne{} achieved the highest mean acceptance ratio, revenue rate, and node utilization among the
compared algorithms. It also maintained the highest mean acceptance ratio across all tested arrival
rates. In particular, its advantage was preserved in the DCNs, where virtual nodes can be placed
only on hosts and insufficient attachment-link bandwidth can make residual host CPU unusable. These
results were obtained with a more restricted search procedure than the RFD-based algorithms.
\ecfvne{} incurred a higher average embedding cost than the RFD-based algorithms but a lower cost
than the ViNE-based algorithms.

The current \ecf{} metric evaluates the local external connectivity of an individual node or link
and therefore does not capture bottlenecks shared by multiple resources. Future work will
investigate computationally tractable methods for detecting such shared bottlenecks without
substantially increasing online embedding time. Another direction is to incorporate \ecf{} into
learning-based VNE methods, for example as part of their state representation, candidate features,
or reward function.
