\begin{abstract}

    In online virtual network embedding (VNE), it is important not only to accommodate the currently
    arriving virtual network request (VNR), but also to preserve the usability of residual substrate
    resources for future requests. Existing approaches mainly consider the absolute amount of
    residual resources or load balancing, but do not directly quantify the extent to which the
    remaining resources can be combined with other resources required to construct future VNR
    embeddings. This paper proposes an External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric
    that quantifies residual-resource usability by combining the residual resource amount with
    external connectivity. The metric is separately defined for substrate nodes and links to reflect
    the different resource relationships required by node embedding and virtual-link routing. We
    further decompose the change in fragmentation before and after an embedding into the effect of
    direct resource consumption and the degradation in the connectivity of the resources that remain
    after the embedding. The latter is defined as the induced fragmentation cost. Based on this
    formulation, VNE-ECF-LB integrates the induced fragmentation cost with a load-balancing cost
    when evaluating node candidates and routing paths. We also consider VNE-ECF, which removes the
    load-balancing term, to isolate the contribution of the proposed fragmentation control.
    Experimental results on synthetic Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi substrates with different connectivity
    densities and real Internet Topology Zoo networks under varying workloads show that VNE-ECF-LB
    consistently improves the VNR acceptance ratio compared with the considered baselines. Although
    a trade-off in the revenue-to-cost ratio is observed under some conditions, the proposed method
    improves long-term VNR admission by continuously utilizing substrate resources while preserving
    their usability for subsequent requests.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern network infrastructures must serve diverse tenants and applications simultaneously. These
services range from latency-sensitive real-time traffic to bandwidth-intensive data transfers, and
their resource requirements are often heterogeneous. Network virtualization addresses these
requirements by abstracting physical infrastructure into virtual resources. Each service request is
commonly modeled as a virtual network request (VNR) with node and link resource requirements.
Mapping a VNR onto the substrate network requires node embedding and virtual-link routing, a process
known as virtual network embedding (VNE)~\cite{satpathy2025virtual}.

In practical deployments, VNE operates in a dynamic setting in which VNRs arrive and depart over
time. Each embedding decision must therefore be made online under changing substrate-resource
availability. This setting is challenging for two reasons. First, VNE is computationally
intractable: the decision version is NP-complete, and optimization variants are
NP-hard~\cite{rost2020hardness}. Practical algorithms therefore rely on heuristic strategies rather
than globally optimal solutions. Second, each locally feasible embedding changes the residual
substrate state available to future requests. As VNRs are repeatedly embedded and released, residual
resources may become distributed in ways that make future embeddings difficult even when sufficient
aggregate capacity remains. This degradation of future resource usability is commonly referred to as
resource fragmentation~\cite{fischer2013virtual}. A locally feasible embedding may therefore serve
the current request while increasing the likelihood that subsequent requests are rejected.

Existing online VNE heuristics manage residual capacity, path feasibility, and embedding cost during
node embedding and virtual-link routing. However, their control granularity is mainly limited to
individual substrate nodes, adjacent link sets, candidate paths, or aggregate load-balancing
objectives. Such approaches do not directly quantify fragmentation in terms of the usability of the
residual substrate resources.

Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_figures} illustrates the resource-fragmentation problem. Fig.~\ref{fig:vn}

substrate network. In terms of aggregate resource capacity, the substrate network may contain
sufficient residual resources to accommodate the request. Nevertheless, the VNR can still be
rejected when these resources cannot be effectively combined to simultaneously satisfy its node and
link requirements.

Thus, the value of a residual resource in VNE is not determined solely by the absolute amount of
remaining CPU or bandwidth. Because a VNR has a graph structure in which nodes and links must be
embedded together, a substrate resource with sufficient residual capacity may still have limited
practical usability if it cannot be effectively combined with surrounding resources for a future
embedding. Resource fragmentation should therefore be evaluated by considering both the amount of
residual resources and their usability. In this work, we represent this usability through external
connectivity and define the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric by combining
external connectivity with the residual resource amount.

Node embedding and virtual-link routing require different forms of resource combination. The
residual CPU of a substrate node must be connected to other resources through the bandwidth of its
incident substrate links. In contrast, the residual bandwidth of a substrate link must be
continuously connected with other links so that it can form a substrate path or a larger embedding
structure. We therefore specialize the same external-connectivity principle to substrate nodes and
links according to their respective roles in VNE.

In addition, a change in the fragmentation score before and after an embedding contains two
different effects: direct resource consumption and the degradation in the connectivity of the
resources that remain. These effects should be distinguished. For example, fragmentation may
decrease simply because an already isolated resource is consumed, whereas an appropriate embedding
may instead preserve the connectivity of the remaining resources and prevent further fragmentation.
We therefore separate these two effects and define only the loss caused by the connectivity
degradation of the remaining resources as the \emph{induced fragmentation cost}.

Based on this formulation, VNE-ECF-LB jointly considers the induced fragmentation cost and a
conventional load-balancing cost when evaluating node candidates and routing paths. The
fragmentation term aims to preserve the combinability of residual resources for future VNRs, while
the load-balancing term discourages excessive concentration of the current demand on individual
substrate resources. For performance evaluation, we additionally consider VNE-ECF, an ablation
variant obtained by removing the load-balancing term from VNE-ECF-LB. This comparison isolates the
effect of the proposed ECF-based fragmentation control and the additional contribution of load
balancing.

The main contributions of this paper are summarized as follows. First, we characterize resource
fragmentation based on the combinability of residual resources rather than solely on their absolute
remaining capacity, and formulate a unified external-connectivity-based \ecf{} metric specialized
for substrate nodes and links. Second, we separate induced fragmentation from the direct effect of
resource consumption and incorporate this cost into online node and path selection through
VNE-ECF-LB, thereby considering both the current embedding and the usability of the substrate
resources left for future requests.
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Resource- and Topology-Aware VNE}

Conventional VNE heuristics commonly prioritize candidate substrate nodes according to residual CPU,
residual bandwidth, node degree, centrality, or combinations of these quantities. PageRank-inspired
and other resource-aware node-ranking heuristics use resource and topology information to estimate
the suitability of substrate nodes, followed by virtual link routing through shortest paths or $k$
shortest paths~\cite{cao2017novel,hashmi2020vne}. Other studies use betweenness centrality,
closeness centrality, degree, strength, and multiple topology attributes to guide node
embedding~\cite{ding2015virtual,guler2016closeness,feng2014topology}. More recent multi-criteria
methods further combine several node attributes into a single ranking score. NORD combines
entropy-based weighting with TOPSIS to rank substrate nodes~\cite{tg2023nord}, while simplified
ELECTRE evaluates candidate substrate nodes using multiple node metrics and
constraints~\cite{zhang2018virtual}. These approaches primarily use resource and topology
information to estimate candidate-node suitability for the current embedding decision rather than to
characterize fragmentation of the residual substrate state.

Another line of work coordinates node embedding and link routing because independently selected node
embeddings can lead to infeasible or costly link routings. Coordinated approaches therefore solve
the two mapping operations jointly or through coupled
stages~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard,lu2023performance,nguyen2022toward}. Related methods extend this
consideration by incorporating congestion, energy consumption, latency, or path characteristics into
embedding decisions~\cite{pham2019congestion,zheng2017link,yan2019latency}. Such coordination
determines how node and link allocation decisions interact and can improve the feasibility or cost
of an embedding. However, coordination and resource fragmentation address different aspects of VNE:
coordination determines how an embedding is constructed, whereas fragmentation metrics characterize
the usability of the residual resources left by that embedding.

Load-balancing approaches address another related aspect by discouraging excessive resource
concentration on particular substrate nodes or
links~\cite{gong2014toward,shanbhag2015vhub,li2022load}. Some load-balancing methods additionally
use global resource information or neighboring bandwidth when evaluating node-embedding potential.
These objectives seek a balanced distribution of substrate utilization. Resource balance and
resource fragmentation, however, are not equivalent. From the fragmentation perspective adopted in
this paper, the relevant question is whether a residual resource can still be used together with the
other resources required by a future mapping operation, rather than only whether residual resources
are evenly distributed across the substrate network. This distinction does not exclude load
balancing; instead, this paper retains load balancing separately through the resource-usage term
$C_{\mathrm{use}}$, while modeling fragmentation cost as a distinct objective.

\subsection{Survivable VNE Under Substrate Failures}

Survivable VNE addresses service availability under substrate failures. Prior studies reserve backup
paths or redundant node and link resources, construct backup topologies, or remap affected virtual
components after
failures~\cite{liao2014survivable,chowdhury2016dedicated,liu2017scheme,zheng2019heuristic,sun2011framework,zhu2014heuristic}.
Shahriar et al.~\cite{shahriar2019virtual} formulate Connectivity-aware Virtual Network Embedding
(CoViNE), which guarantees VN connectivity under multiple substrate link failures without requiring
full bandwidth protection. CoViNE uses VN augmentation and disjointness requirements to preserve
connectivity after failures. Survivable VNE concerns whether an embedded VN can continue to satisfy
a survivability requirement after substrate failures, whereas this paper considers the usability of
residual resources resulting from online embedding decisions under normal operation.

\subsection{Fragmentation-Aware VNE}

The following studies are most closely related to the fragmentation notion considered in this paper.
Fragmentation-aware VNE explicitly considers residual resources that become difficult to use despite
remaining available.

Guan et al.~\cite{guan2023multidimensional} study multidimensional resource fragmentation for VNE in
MEC systems interconnected by optical networks. For physical nodes, their formulation characterizes
fragmentation through imbalanced utilization of computing and wireless resources. For physical
links, it identifies unusable or difficult-to-use optical spectrum fragments created under spectrum
continuity and consistency constraints. Their bilevel formulation also accounts for the dependency
between virtual node embedding and virtual link routing. Thus, their fragmentation model focuses on
imbalance among heterogeneous node resources and contiguity of optical spectrum resources, which
differs from the CPU--bandwidth resource relationships addressed in this paper. Whereas this paper
treats node and link fragmentation as two instances of the same general form $F=R(1-\mathrm{Conn})$,
Guan et al. formulate two problems with different characteristics: node imbalance and link spectrum
continuity.

Lu and Zhang~\cite{lu2022resource} address a more closely related notion of resource fragmentation.
They define the resource fragmentation degree (RFD) for substrate nodes and links based on the
observation that the usability of a residual resource depends on surrounding residual resources. For
a substrate node, RFD incorporates residual resources of neighboring nodes and paths within a
bounded hop count. For a substrate link, RFD incorporates the residual resources of adjacent links
and the substrate nodes shared by those links. Their formulation is motivated by an extension of
graph connectivity and aggregates these surrounding resource states to quantify the fragmentation
degree of each substrate element. This connectivity extension depends on a hop-bounded neighborhood
and its associated hyperparameter, whereas the external connectivity used in this paper is justified
directly by the boundary cut, equivalently the max-flow between a resource region and its exterior,
and requires no hop-count tuning.

The proposed metric shares with RFD the observation that residual resource amount alone is
insufficient to characterize future usability. The distinction lies in how the relevant resource
relationships are modeled. Rather than deriving a connectivity-inspired fragmentation degree from
surrounding nodes and paths, the proposed metric directly follows the resource combinations required
by the two VNE mapping operations. For node embedding, it evaluates the residual resource of a
substrate node together with the residual resources of its adjacent substrate links, because both
are required to support a virtual node and its incident virtual links. For link routing, it
evaluates the residual resource of a substrate link together with the adjacent link resources at
each of its two endpoints. The two endpoint states are evaluated separately, and the endpoint with
lower adjacent-resource availability determines the resulting value because insufficient adjacent
resources at either endpoint can limit the use of the substrate link as part of a path.

Accordingly, the proposed metric differs from the reviewed fragmentation metrics not in whether
neighboring resources are considered, but in how their relationships are defined. It characterizes
residual-resource usability according to the distinct resource combinations required by node
embedding and link routing, aiming to identify residual resources that remain available but are
difficult to combine with other resources in future VNR embeddings. In addition, the formulation
distinguishes the direct resource-consumption effect of an embedding from the connectivity
degradation it induces, with the latter represented through the external connectivity metric
introduced in the next section.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system_model}

We model the substrate network as a weighted undirected graph $G^S=(N^S,L^S)$, where $N^S$ and $L^S$
denote the sets of substrate nodes and substrate links, respectively. For each substrate node $v\in
N^S$, we let $c_v$ and $r_v$ denote its initial and current residual CPU capacities, and for each
substrate link $e=\{u,v\}\in L^S$, we let $c_e$ and $r_e$ denote its initial and current residual
bandwidth capacities. To represent paths and flows for virtual-link routing, we expand each
undirected substrate link $\{u,v\}\in L^S$ into two directed arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$, and we
collect these arcs into the set $A^S$.

We represent a VNR as a weighted undirected graph $G^V=(N^V,L^V)$. Here $N^V$ and $L^V$ are the sets
of virtual nodes and virtual links, respectively; we let $d_n$ denote the CPU demand of virtual node
$n\in N^V$, and $d_l$ the bandwidth demand of virtual link $l=\{n,m\}\in L^V$.

We define the node mapping as $M_N:N^V\rightarrow N^S$. Each virtual node is mapped to exactly one
substrate node, and we assume that distinct virtual nodes of the same VNR are mapped to distinct
substrate nodes. We use the binary variable $x_{nv}\in\{0,1\}$, where $x_{nv}=1$ indicates that
virtual node $n$ is mapped to substrate node $v$. We define the link mapping as
$M_L:L^V\rightarrow\mathcal{P}^S$, where each virtual link $l=\{n,m\}$ is mapped to substrate paths
connecting $M_N(n)$ and $M_N(m)$. We let $f_{uv}^l\ge0$ denote the bandwidth flow of virtual link
$l$ that traverses arc $(u,v)\in A^S$.

We define the complete VNR embedding as $M=(M_N,M_L)$. After applying an embedding $M$, we update
the residual resources of substrate nodes and substrate links, respectively, as follows.
\[
    r_v(M)
    =
    r_v-\sum_{n\in N^V}d_nx_{nv},
    \qquad \forall v\in N^S,
\]
\[
    r_{\{u,v\}}(M)
    =
    r_{\{u,v\}}
    -
    \sum_{l\in L^V}
    \left(
    f_{uv}^l+f_{vu}^l
    \right),
    \qquad
    \forall\{u,v\}\in L^S.
\]
The pre- and post-embedding residual substrate states defined above serve as the basis for computing
the \ecf{} metric and the induced fragmentation cost in the following sections.

\section{External-Connectivity-Based Fragmentation Metric}
\label{sec:metric_definition_of_resource_fragmentation}

We now define the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf) metric, which combines residual
amount and external connectivity to represent the future usability of a residual resource. The
\ecf{} metric uses a common base form for nodes and links, but specializes to reflect the different
ways in which each resource combines with other resources in VNE.

\subsection{Base Definition and External Connectivity}
In VNE, the usability of a residual resource depends not only on its amount but also on its
combinability with surrounding resources. Let $R$ denote the residual amount and $\mathrm{Conn}$ the
normalized external connectivity; we view $R\cdot\mathrm{Conn}$ as the effectively usable portion of
the residual resource that can combine with external resources. We accordingly define the
fragmentation score as
\[
    F=R(1-\mathrm{Conn}).
\]
When $\mathrm{Conn}$ approaches one, the residual resource can be combined with external resources
sufficiently and fragmentation is small; in contrast, as external connectivity decreases, the
fragmentation score grows for the same residual amount.

To make external connectivity concrete, we consider any $\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq N^S$ and let
$\bar A=N^S\setminus A$. We define the boundary link set that connects the region $A$ with its
exterior $\bar A$ as
\[
    \delta(A)
    =
    \{\,e=\{u,v\}\in L^S
    \mid
    u\in A,\ v\in\bar A\,\}.
\]
We define the initial and current bandwidth capacities of this boundary as
\[
    B_{\mathrm{cap}}(A)
    =
    \sum_{e\in\delta(A)}c_e,
    \qquad
    B_{\mathrm{res}}(A)
    =
    \sum_{e\in\delta(A)}r_e,
\]
and we define the normalized external connectivity as
\[
    \mathrm{Conn}(A)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{B_{\mathrm{res}}(A)}{B_{\mathrm{cap}}(A)},
         & B_{\mathrm{cap}}(A)>0, \\[1mm]
        0,
         & B_{\mathrm{cap}}(A)=0.
    \end{cases}
\]
If we connect $A$ and $\bar A$ to a super source and a super sink, respectively, the cut that
separates the two regions coincides with $\delta(A)$; the boundary capacity therefore represents the
capacity available for region $A$ to connect with its exterior.

Two properties motivate this specific choice of connectivity term. First, $\mathrm{Conn}(A)$ is a
normalized max-flow upper bound between $A$ and $\bar A$: by the max-flow min-cut theorem, any
single-commodity flow from $A$ to $\bar A$ cannot exceed $B_{\mathrm{res}}(A)$. Thus, if
$\mathrm{Conn}(A)= 0$, region $A$ cannot support any additional external combination regardless of
how the demand is routed. Second, $\mathrm{Conn}$ is monotone under embedding: because every
embedding only removes bandwidth from $\delta(A)$, we have $B_{\mathrm{res}}(A;M)\le
B_{\mathrm{res}}(A)$ and therefore $\mathrm{Conn}(A;M)\le\mathrm{Conn}(A)$. Consequently
$I(A;M)-I(A)\ge0$ for every $A$, so the induced fragmentation cost is non-negative by construction.
These two properties keep the metric interpretable as a resource-usability bound and ensure that the
induced fragmentation cost behaves consistently under embedding.

\subsection{Node Fragmentation}
For a substrate node $v\in N^S$, we set $A=\{v\}$, so that the external connectivity of the node is
given by
\[
    \mathrm{Conn}_N(v)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{\sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}r_e}
        {\sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}c_e},
         & \sum_{e\in\delta(\{v\})}c_e>0, \\[1mm]
        0,
         & \text{otherwise.}
    \end{cases}
\]
In VNE, the residual CPU of a substrate node must combine with the bandwidth of its adjacent links
to reach other substrate resources; therefore, a reduction in adjacent bandwidth lowers the future
usability of that CPU. Accordingly, we define the node connectivity-deficiency factor and the node
fragmentation score as
\[
    I_N(v)=1-\mathrm{Conn}_N(v),
    \qquad
    F_N(v)=r_v I_N(v).
\]

\subsection{Link Fragmentation}
For the residual bandwidth of a substrate link $e=\{u,v\}$ to be usable for virtual-link routing, it
must connect with other substrate links at both endpoints so as to form a continuous substrate path.
Because the residual bandwidth of the central link $e$ itself is captured directly through $r_e$, we
exclude $e$ itself when computing the external connectivity at its endpoints. That is, we define
\[
    \delta(u\mid e)
    =
    \delta(\{u\})\setminus\{e\},
    \qquad
    \delta(v\mid e)
    =
    \delta(\{v\})\setminus\{e\}.
\]
The external connectivity at endpoint $u$ is then
\[
    \mathrm{Conn}(u\mid e)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{\sum_{q\in\delta(u\mid e)}r_q}
        {\sum_{q\in\delta(u\mid e)}c_q},
         & \sum_{q\in\delta(u\mid e)}c_q>0, \\[1mm]
        0,
         & \text{otherwise,}
    \end{cases}
\]
and we define $\mathrm{Conn}(v\mid e)$ analogously.

For link $e$ to be used as part of a substrate path, connectivity to a subsequent link is required
at both endpoints; we therefore treat the endpoint with lower external connectivity as the one that
limits the usability of the link. Accordingly, we define
\[
    \mathrm{Conn}_L(e)
    =
    \min
    \left\{
    \mathrm{Conn}(u\mid e),
    \mathrm{Conn}(v\mid e)
    \right\},
\]
and we define the link connectivity-deficiency factor and the link fragmentation score as
\[
    I_L(e)=1-\mathrm{Conn}_L(e),
    \qquad
    F_L(e)=r_e I_L(e).
\]

We aggregate the two endpoint connectivities with a $\min$ rather than an average or a product for
two reasons. Structurally, extending $e$ into a substrate path requires a further link at both
endpoints, so a path can be formed only if both $\mathrm{Conn}(u\mid e)$ and $\mathrm{Conn}(v\mid
e)$ remain positive; a $\min$ preserves this bottleneck semantics. Empirically, we compared $\min$,
arithmetic mean, and geometric mean in a pilot run on ER $G(50,0.15)$ and observed that $\min$
produced the highest acceptance ratio, with the geometric mean second and the arithmetic mean last;
the ordering matches the intuition that a link's usability is limited by its weakest endpoint. We
therefore keep $\min$ as the default aggregation and treat other choices as a hyperparameter that
can be revisited when both endpoints are known to carry symmetric load.

\subsection{Induced Fragmentation Cost}
The difference between the pre- and post-embedding fragmentation scores contains both the direct
effect of resources being consumed and the effect of the connectivity of the remaining resources
changing after that consumption. For a general fragmentation score $F=RI$, we decompose the change
caused by embedding $M$ as
\[
    F(M)-F
    =
    \bigl(R(M)-R\bigr)I
    +
    R(M)\bigl(I(M)-I\bigr).
\]
The first term captures the direct consumption effect from the reduction in the resource amount
itself, while the second term captures the additional fragmentation effect caused by the degraded
connectivity of the remaining resources after embedding. Because our objective is to measure the
loss of usability of the resources that remain, we retain only the second term as induced
fragmentation.

We accordingly define the induced fragmentation cost of nodes and links as
\[
    \Delta F_N(M)
    =
    \sum_{v\in N^S}
    r_v(M)
    \left(
    I_N(v;M)-I_N(v)
    \right),
\]
\[
    \Delta F_L(M)
    =
    \sum_{e\in L^S}
    r_e(M)
    \left(
    I_L(e;M)-I_L(e)
    \right),
\]
To make node and link induced-fragmentation costs comparable in a single scalar objective, we
normalize each term by the corresponding substrate-wide capacity budget. Let
$C_{N,\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{v\in N^S}c_v$ denote the total initial CPU capacity of the substrate and
$C_{L,\mathrm{tot}}=\sum_{e\in L^S}c_e$ the total initial bandwidth capacity. The normalized
induced-fragmentation costs are then
\[
    \widetilde{\Delta F}_N(M)=\frac{\Delta F_N(M)}{C_{N,\mathrm{tot}}},
    \qquad
    \widetilde{\Delta F}_L(M)=\frac{\Delta F_L(M)}{C_{L,\mathrm{tot}}},
\]
both of which are dimensionless and lie in $[0,1]$ under the monotonicity property of
Section~\ref{sec:metric_definition_of_resource_fragmentation}. We define the overall induced
fragmentation cost as
\[
    \Delta F_{\mathrm{frag}}(M)
    =
    \widetilde{\Delta F}_L(M)
    +
    \gamma\,\widetilde{\Delta F}_N(M),
    \qquad
    \gamma\ge0,
\]
where $\gamma$ controls the relative weight between node and link fragmentation. Because both
normalized terms share the same $[0,1]$ scale, $\gamma$ carries a clear ratio interpretation rather
than absorbing the CPU-to-bandwidth unit gap.


\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

The goal of \ecfvne{} is to satisfy the resource requirements of the current VNR without
concentrating them on particular substrate resources, while at the same time preserving the
usability of the substrate resources that remain after embedding. To this end, we compose the
conventional load-balancing cost and the \ecf-based induced fragmentation cost from the previous
section as independent terms and combine them into a single objective function.

The decision variable $x_{nv}\in\{0,1\}$ indicates whether virtual node $n\in N^V$ is mapped to
substrate node $v\in N^S$, and $f_{uv}^l\ge0$ denotes the portion of the bandwidth demand of virtual
link $l\in L^V$ that traverses arc $(u,v)\in A^S$.

\subsection{Embedding Constraints}
Each virtual node must be mapped to exactly one substrate node, and to prevent virtual nodes of the
same VNR from being mapped to the same substrate node, we impose
\[
    \sum_{v\in N^S}x_{nv}=1,
    \qquad
    \forall n\in N^V,
\]
\[
    \sum_{n\in N^V}x_{nv}\le1,
    \qquad
    \forall v\in N^S.
\]
In addition, the total CPU demand allocated by the node mapping must not exceed the current residual
CPU of each substrate node:
\[
    \sum_{n\in N^V}d_nx_{nv}
    \le r_v,
    \qquad
    \forall v\in N^S.
\]

For virtual-link routing, the sum of flows traversing a substrate link in both directions must not
exceed its residual bandwidth:
\[
    \sum_{l\in L^V}
    \left(
    f_{uv}^l+f_{vu}^l
    \right)
    \le r_{\{u,v\}},
    \qquad
    \forall\{u,v\}\in L^S.
\]
The source and destination of each virtual link $l=\{n,m\}\in L^V$ are determined by the node
mapping, and we express this via the flow-conservation constraint
\[
    \sum_{(v,w)\in A^S}f_{vw}^l
    -
    \sum_{(w,v)\in A^S}f_{wv}^l
    =
    d_l
    \left(
    x_{nv}-x_{mv}
    \right),
    \qquad
    \forall v\in N^S.
\]
Thus, a flow of $d_l$ is generated at the substrate node with $x_{nv}=1$ and absorbed in equal
amount at the substrate node with $x_{mv}=1$.

\subsection{Objective Function}
External connectivity evaluates how well a residual resource can combine with surrounding resources,
but it does not directly represent the absolute residual capacity of an individual substrate
resource or the load concentration on a particular resource. To complement this, we adopt the
following demand-to-residual-capacity load-balancing cost ~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard}:
\[
    C_{\mathrm{LB}}(x,f)
    =
    \sum_{n\in N^V}
    \sum_{v\in N^S}
    \frac{d_n}{r_v+\epsilon}x_{nv}
    +
    \sum_{l\in L^V}
    \sum_{(u,v)\in A^S}
    \frac{f_{uv}^l}
    {r_{\{u,v\}}+\epsilon},
\]
where $\epsilon>0$ is a small constant that prevents division by zero. Assigning the same demand to
a resource with smaller residual capacity incurs a larger cost, thereby discouraging load
concentration on heavily utilized substrate resources.

For the formulation below, we write $\Delta F_{\mathrm{frag}}(x,f)$ for the induced fragmentation
cost resulting from the embedding specified by the decision variables $x$ and $f$.

We define the overall objective by combining load balancing and normalized induced fragmentation as
\[
    \min_{x,f}
    \quad
    J(x,f)
    =
    \beta\,C_{\mathrm{LB}}(x,f)
    +
    \lambda\,
    \Delta F_{\mathrm{frag}}(x,f),
    \qquad
    \beta,\lambda\ge0.
\]
Here $\beta$ and $\lambda$ control the relative contributions of load balancing and induced
fragmentation, respectively, while $\gamma$ controls the relative contribution of node fragmentation
within the fragmentation term. In this way, \ecfvne{} jointly considers load concentration for the
current request and the usability of residual substrate resources after embedding.

\section{ECF-VNE Embedding Algorithm}
\label{sec:embedding_algorithm}

The proposed \ecfvne{} method is implemented within the online embedding framework of
VNE-RFD~\cite{lu2022resource}. We retain its virtual-node processing order, feasible substrate-node
candidate generation, $K$-shortest-path routing, and bounded backtracking procedure. The proposed
method changes only the criterion used to evaluate a tentative embedding. Each tentative embedding
$M$ generated by the underlying framework is evaluated according to both its immediate resource
usage and the induced fragmentation of the residual substrate resources.

The load-balancing component evaluates the resources directly consumed by $M$. For a mapped virtual
node, the CPU demand is weighted by the residual CPU of its substrate node before the tentative
embedding. Likewise, for a routed virtual link, its bandwidth demand is accumulated over the
substrate path assigned by $M_L$. This corresponds to the load-balancing cost defined in
Section~\ref{sec:problem_formulation}, expressed here directly in terms of the node and path
mappings contained in $M$.

The fragmentation component is evaluated from the residual substrate state before and after applying
$M$. Importantly, evaluating the induced fragmentation does not require treating every substrate
resource as affected by every tentative embedding. The node connectivity-deficiency factor of a
substrate node changes only when the residual bandwidth of at least one of its incident substrate
links changes. Therefore, node induced fragmentation needs to be reevaluated only for substrate
nodes adjacent to links whose bandwidth is consumed by $M$.

A similar locality property applies to substrate links. The connectivity-deficiency factor of a
substrate link depends on the residual bandwidth of the other links incident to its two endpoints.
Consequently, a substrate link needs to be reevaluated only when $M$ changes the residual bandwidth
of another link adjacent to either of its endpoints. The bandwidth consumption of a link does not by
itself increase its own connectivity deficiency because the central link is excluded from the
endpoint external-connectivity calculation. Instead, that consumption can reduce the external
connectivity of neighboring substrate resources.

After identifying these affected resources, \ecfvne{} evaluates their connectivity-deficiency
factors before and after $M$. For each affected substrate node or link, only the increase in
connectivity deficiency of the resource remaining after the tentative embedding contributes to
induced fragmentation. Resources whose connectivity deficiency is unchanged make no contribution,
even if part of their capacity is consumed. This preserves the distinction between direct resource
consumption and fragmentation induced in the remaining substrate resources.

The resulting node and link induced-fragmentation costs are normalized by the corresponding
substrate-wide capacity budgets and combined according to the definition of $\Delta
F_{\mathrm{frag}}(M)$. The final evaluation of $M$ then combines this value with its load-balancing
cost using the same weights as the objective function in Section~\ref{sec:problem_formulation}. The
underlying VNE-RFD framework uses this evaluation value to rank its tentative node and path choices;
candidate generation, commitment, rollback, and bounded backtracking remain unchanged.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecf_eval} summarizes the evaluation of a tentative embedding $M$.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
    \caption{ECF-Based Tentative Embedding Evaluation}
    \label{alg:ecf_eval}
    \footnotesize
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]

        \STATE \textbf{Input:} $G^S$, $G^V$, tentative embedding $M$, $\beta$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$,
        $\epsilon$

        \STATE \textbf{Output:} evaluation value of $M$

        \STATE $C_{\mathrm{LB}}\leftarrow 0$

        \FOR{each $n\in N^V$ mapped by $M_N$} \STATE $v\leftarrow M_N(n)$ \STATE $C_{\mathrm{LB}}
            \leftarrow
            C_{\mathrm{LB}} + \dfrac{d_n}{r_v+\epsilon}$ \ENDFOR

        \FOR{each $l\in L^V$ routed by $M_L$} \FOR{each $e\in M_L(l)$} \STATE $C_{\mathrm{LB}}
            \leftarrow
            C_{\mathrm{LB}} + \dfrac{d_l}{r_e+\epsilon}$ \ENDFOR \ENDFOR

        \STATE Obtain $r_v(M)$ and $r_e(M)$ after tentatively applying $M$

        \STATE $\Delta F_N(M)\leftarrow0$

        \FOR{each $v\in N^S$ incident to a link $e$ with $r_e(M)<r_e$} \STATE Evaluate $I_N(v)$
        using the current residual bandwidths \STATE Evaluate $I_N(v;M)$ using the residual
        bandwidths after $M$ \STATE $\Delta F_N(M)
            \leftarrow
            \Delta F_N(M) + r_v(M) \bigl(I_N(v;M)-I_N(v)\bigr)$ \ENDFOR

        \STATE $\Delta F_L(M)\leftarrow0$

        \FOR{each $e={u,v}\in L^S$} \IF{there exists $q\in\delta(u\mid e)\cup\delta(v\mid e)$ with
        $r_q(M)<r_q$}


        \STATE Evaluate $I_L(e)$ using the current residual bandwidths \STATE Evaluate $I_L(e;M)$
        using the residual bandwidths after $M$

        \STATE $\Delta F_L(M)
            \leftarrow
            \Delta F_L(M) + r_e(M) \bigl(I_L(e;M)-I_L(e)\bigr)$ \ENDIF


        \ENDFOR

        \STATE $\Delta F_{\mathrm{frag}}(M)
            \leftarrow
            \dfrac{\Delta F_L(M)}{C_{L,\mathrm{tot}}} +
            \gamma
            \dfrac{\Delta F_N(M)}{C_{N,\mathrm{tot}}}$

        \STATE
        \textbf{return}
        $\beta C_{\mathrm{LB}} + \lambda\Delta F_{\mathrm{frag}}(M)$

    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The evaluation in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ecf_eval} is invoked whenever the underlying VNE-RFD framework
compares tentative embedding choices. Because the input is the tentative embedding $M$ itself, the
ECF evaluation does not maintain separate intermediate substrate states for individual node or link
decisions. The internal processing order of a request remains the responsibility of the underlying
embedding framework, while the proposed algorithm evaluates the residual-resource consequence of the
resulting tentative embedding.

The load-balancing part of the evaluation is linear in the number of substrate resources used by
$M$. For induced fragmentation, node connectivity needs to be reevaluated only around substrate
links whose residual bandwidth changes. Link connectivity likewise needs to be reevaluated only for
links whose endpoint neighborhoods contain such changed links. In the worst case, when the tentative
embedding affects the substrate widely, evaluating all node connectivities requires $O(|L^S|)$
operations, while evaluating all link connectivities requires $O(\sum_{v\in N^S}(d_v^S)^2)$
operations. Thus, the worst-case complexity of one induced-fragmentation evaluation is $O(\sum_{v\in
N^S}(d_v^S)^2)$, which is contained in $O(|N^S||L^S|)$. For localized tentative embeddings, only the
affected neighborhoods need to be revisited, reducing the practical cost of each evaluation.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
\label{sec:performance_evaluation}

\subsection{Simulation Setup and Evaluation Metrics}

We evaluate the online embedding performance of \ecfvne{} against VNE-ECF, VNE-RFD-B and
VNE-RFD-D~\cite{lu2022resource}, and D-ViNE-LB and R-ViNE-LB~\cite{chowdhury2012vineyard}. Here,
\ecfvne{} is the proposed method that jointly considers the external-connectivity-based
fragmentation cost and the load-balancing cost. VNE-ECF is an ablation variant in which the
load-balancing term is removed, and is included to isolate the contribution of load balancing when
combined with the proposed ECF metric.

The CPU capacity of each substrate node and the bandwidth capacity of each substrate link are
independently generated from $U(50,100)$. Each VNR is generated as a connected Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi
graph $G(n,0.4)$ containing 2--10 virtual nodes. The CPU demand of each virtual node is generated
from $U(0,20)$, while the bandwidth demand of each virtual link is generated from $U(0,50)$.

VNRs arrive according to a Poisson process, and their holding times follow an exponential
distribution with a mean of 1,000 time units. For each simulation, the initial transient period is
discarded, and the performance metrics are collected over a steady-state interval of 50,000 time
units. All compared methods are evaluated under the same paired-sampling setting.

The primary performance metric is the VNR acceptance ratio, which measures the fraction of arriving
VNRs that can be successfully embedded over time. We additionally measure the revenue-to-cost ratio,
average CPU utilization, and average link utilization. These metrics are considered together to
examine whether an improvement in acceptance ratio is accompanied by changes in embedding efficiency
or substrate-resource utilization.

\subsection{Performance under Different ER Substrate Densities}

For the synthetic-substrate experiments, we use a connected Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi graph $G(50,p_s)$
with 50 substrate nodes and vary the edge probability as $p_s\in\{0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25\}$. The
VNR arrival rate is fixed at 50 requests per 1,000 time units. This experiment examines how changes
in substrate connectivity affect the performance of the embedding algorithms.

Figs.~\ref{fig:er_acceptance}--\ref{fig:er_link_utilization} show the four performance metrics as
$p_s$ varies. As $p_s$ increases, the substrate contains more links and provides a larger number of
feasible routing paths. Consequently, the acceptance ratio generally increases for all compared
algorithms. Nevertheless, \ecfvne{} achieves the highest acceptance ratio over the entire range of
$p_s$.

Compared with the best-performing external baseline at each substrate density, \ecfvne{} improves
the VNR acceptance ratio by approximately 11.9\%--63.2\%. The relative gain is particularly
pronounced when the substrate is sparse, where preserving the combinability of the remaining
resources is more important because fewer alternative routing paths are available. Even as the
substrate becomes denser and path diversity increases, \ecfvne{} continues to maintain the highest
acceptance ratio.

The comparison with VNE-ECF further shows the effect of combining the proposed fragmentation metric
with load balancing. Across the tested values of $p_s$, \ecfvne{} improves the acceptance ratio over
VNE-ECF by approximately 2.7\%--8.7\%. This indicates that external connectivity and load balancing
play complementary roles: the ECF term discourages embeddings that degrade the usability of residual
resources, while the load-balancing term prevents excessive concentration of the current demand on
individual substrate resources.

Fig.~\ref{fig:er_revenue_cost} shows that the higher acceptance ratio does not necessarily imply a
uniformly higher revenue-to-cost ratio under all substrate densities. At the two sparsest settings,
\ecfvne{} has a revenue-to-cost ratio approximately 5.1\% lower than VNE-RFD-D. As the substrate
becomes denser, however, this relation is reversed, and \ecfvne{} achieves an improvement of up to
approximately 24.5\% over VNE-RFD-D. Thus, the acceptance-ratio improvement cannot be attributed
simply to accepting requests through increasingly expensive embeddings.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:er_cpu_utilization}, \ecfvne{} generally exhibits higher average CPU
utilization because it continuously accepts more VNRs and therefore places more virtual-node demand
on the substrate. In contrast, the average link utilization in Fig.~\ref{fig:er_link_utilization}
does not necessarily follow the same trend as the acceptance ratio. As $p_s$ increases, the number
of available substrate links and alternative routing paths also increases, allowing accepted
virtual-link traffic to be distributed over a larger set of substrate resources. These results
indicate that the higher acceptance ratio of \ecfvne{} is associated with sustained use of the
substrate resources rather than simple concentration of link usage.

\subsection{Performance on Real Topology Zoo Networks}

To examine whether the observations from the synthetic ER substrates are specific to random graph
structures, we further evaluate the algorithms using real network topologies obtained from the
Internet Topology Zoo~\cite{knight2011internet}. Parallel edges in the original topologies are
merged to construct simple graphs. We select Palmetto $(45,64)$, Bell Canada $(48,64)$, and Surfnet
$(50,68)$, where each pair denotes the numbers of substrate nodes and links, respectively. The three
networks have similar sizes, which reduces the effect of network scale and allows the robustness of
\ecfvne{} to be evaluated across different real connectivity structures.

The substrate-resource distributions, VNR generation procedure, holding-time distribution,
simulation duration, and paired-sampling setting are identical to those used in the ER experiments.
In this experiment, the VNR arrival rate is varied among 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 requests per 1,000
time units to evaluate performance as the offered workload increases.

Fig.~\ref{fig:zoo} shows the results for the three real topologies. For all topologies, the VNR
acceptance ratio decreases as the arrival rate increases because of increasing contention for the
available substrate resources. Despite this increased contention, \ecfvne{} consistently achieves
the highest acceptance ratio under every tested topology and arrival-rate condition.

Among the external baselines, VNE-RFD-B provides the highest acceptance ratio throughout these
experiments. Relative to VNE-RFD-B, \ecfvne{} improves the acceptance ratio by approximately
60.9\%--68.9\% on Palmetto, 66.5\%--70.6\% on Bell Canada, and 68.4\%--71.3\% on Surfnet. The
consistent gains across the three networks indicate that the benefit observed in the ER $p_s$-sweep
is not restricted to a particular random-topology model. Instead, preserving external connectivity
also improves the usability of residual resources under heterogeneous real network structures.

The ablation results further demonstrate the contribution of the load-balancing term. Compared with
VNE-ECF, \ecfvne{} improves the acceptance ratio by approximately 21.2\%--25.1\% on Palmetto,
28.3\%--34.9\% on Bell Canada, and 25.4\%--35.6\% on Surfnet. The substantially larger difference
than that observed in the ER density experiment suggests that jointly controlling fragmentation and
resource concentration becomes particularly important under the connectivity structures of these
real networks.

A trade-off is observed in the revenue-to-cost ratio. VNE-RFD-D achieves a higher ratio under the
tested Topology Zoo conditions, while the revenue-to-cost ratio of \ecfvne{} is approximately
3.6\%--9.3\% lower depending on the topology and workload. At the same time, \ecfvne{} accepts
substantially more VNRs and therefore exhibits higher CPU utilization. This indicates that the
proposed method favors sustained admission and utilization of substrate resources rather than
maximizing the efficiency of each individual accepted embedding.

Average link utilization varies with both topology and workload and does not exhibit the same
ordering as the acceptance ratio. This is expected because the amount and distribution of available
routing capacity differ across the three real networks. More importantly, the relative
acceptance-ratio advantage of \ecfvne{} is maintained across all three topologies and all tested
workload levels. These results support the proposed residual-resource-usability perspective:
embedding decisions that jointly account for external-connectivity- based fragmentation and load
balancing preserve substrate resources in forms that remain more usable for subsequent VNRs.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper addressed the resource-fragmentation problem in online VNE, where residual substrate
resources may remain sufficient in aggregate but become difficult to use for future requests because
they cannot be effectively combined with other residual resources. To characterize this loss of
usability, we introduced the External-Connectivity-based Fragmentation (\ecf{}) metric, which
jointly considers the residual resource amount and its external connectivity. The metric is
specialized for substrate nodes and links to reflect the different resource relationships required
by node embedding and virtual-link routing.

We further separated the change in fragmentation caused by direct resource consumption from the
degradation in the connectivity of the resources that remain after an embedding. The latter was
defined as the induced fragmentation cost. Based on this formulation, \ecfvne{} jointly considers
the induced fragmentation cost and a load-balancing cost when evaluating embedding decisions. We
also evaluated VNE-ECF, an ablation variant that removes the load-balancing term, to distinguish the
contribution of external-connectivity-based fragmentation control from the additional effect of load
balancing.

Experiments on synthetic Erd\H{o}s--R\'{e}nyi substrates with different connectivity densities and
on real Internet Topology Zoo networks under varying VNR arrival rates showed that \ecfvne{}
consistently achieved higher VNR acceptance ratios than the compared methods. The comparison with
VNE-ECF also showed that combining ECF-based fragmentation control with load balancing further
improves online embedding performance. Although a trade-off in revenue-to-cost ratio was observed
under some conditions, \ecfvne{} was able to admit more VNRs while sustaining substrate-resource
utilization, which is consistent with the objective of preserving residual resources for future
requests.

The proposed metric evaluates residual-resource usability through local external connectivity around
each substrate node or link. Therefore, it does not directly capture resource-sharing relationships
among multiple outward paths. In particular, different boundary links may later share the same
substrate link or bottleneck region, in which case high local external connectivity does not
necessarily imply high independent embedding capacity. Thus, while the proposed metric provides an
efficient measure of local residual-resource usability, it does not fully represent dependencies
caused by global path overlap or shared bottlenecks.

Future work may extend the external-connectivity formulation by incorporating path overlap, shared
bottlenecks, or broader flow- and cut-based structures. An important direction is to capture both
local resource connectivity and global resource-sharing relationships while retaining a
computational cost suitable for repeated evaluation in online VNE.
